### Moral foundations {#moral-foundations}
The trolley problem has been used in moral psychology for its ability to capture both cognitive and emotional approaches to moral judgment. In the switch dilemma, a runaway trolley is headed for five workers who will be killed if it proceeds on its present course (Thomson, 1985). To save them, one must throw a switch, turning the trolley so it will kill one person instead of five. Research shows most people find this acceptable (e.g., Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley, & Cohen, 2001). In the footbridge dilemma, a trolley again threatens to kill five people. This time, the actor is on a footbridge next to a large stranger (Thomson, 1985). To save them, one must push the stranger off the bridge, onto the tracks. This will kill the stranger, but save the five workers. Research shows most people find this unacceptable (e.g., Greene et al., 2001). Although various theories attempt to account for these discrepancies, we wanted to investigate the moral antecedents of trolley dilemma responses. That is, we wanted to find out which moral principles people rely on when making decisions about the two versions of the trolley dilemma.

Participants completed materials online and were randomly assigned to either the switch or footbridge dilemma. After deciding the moral permissibility of the action, participants completed the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ; Graham, Haidt, & Nosek, 2009) before completing the demographic questionnaire. A hierarchical regression analysis was ran to assess which of the MFQ scales predicted responses to the switch or footbridge dilemma. We entered the condition, individualizing foundations score, and binding foundations score in Step 1. We then entered the individualizing foundations by condition interaction term and the binding foundations by condition interaction term into Step 2.

Results showed the condition and individualizing foundations explained 28.4% of the variance, F(3, 111) = 14.69, p < .001 R2 = .28. The condition (β = .45, p < .001) and individualizing foundations (β = -.29, p = .01) predicted responses to the trolley problem. The switch condition predicted more endorsement of utilitarian responses to the trolley dilemmas; however, high individualizing scores predicted rejection of utilitarian responses.

Findings suggest those who make utilitarian judgments in these dilemmas rely on the individualizing foundations, indicating different responses to the trolley problem rely on different moral foundations and providing a clearer picture of the moral underpinnings of the trolley problem.
